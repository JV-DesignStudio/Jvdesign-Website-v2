What Is Cyber Crime?
Define cyber crime and the reasons people commit it
Locked
🎯
Goal for this step

Define the term cyber crime, and explain the possible motives for cyber crime.

What is cyber crime?

Cyber crime is any crime where a computer, network or the internet is used as the tool, as the target, or as both. It can be run by one person or a large group, and it can happen to anyone: a person, a business, or a whole country.

Common examples include:

  • 1Hacking getting into a system that is not yours
  • 2Malware viruses, spyware and ransomware
  • 3Phishing fake emails or messages that trick you into giving details
  • 4Identity theft pretending to be someone else to steal money or data
  • 5Online fraud scams, fake shops and stolen card details

Why do people do it? (motives)

Most cyber crime is about money, but it is not the only reason. Attackers are often motivated by:

  • 1Financial gain stealing money, selling data, or demanding a ransom
  • 2Espionage stealing secrets from a company or a country
  • 3Ideology or activism pushing a political or moral message
  • 4Thrill or status proving they can, or earning respect in a group
  • 5Revenge lashing out at a person or company they are angry with
🦊
Lumo says: a motive is the reason behind the crime. If you can name the motive, you can often predict the kind of attacker you are dealing with.

Who Are the Threat Actors?
Individuals, companies, nations, hackers and hacktivists
Locked
🎯
Goal for this step

Identify potential threat actors and understand what each one usually wants.

A threat actor is the person or group who carries out a cyber attack. Knowing the type of threat actor helps you guess their motive and how they work.

  • 1An individual a lone attacker, often working alone for money or the challenge
  • 2A company one business spying on another to steal ideas or customers
  • 3A nation a country attacking another country, its government or its infrastructure
  • 4A hacker someone with strong technical skills who breaks into systems
  • 5A hacktivist an attacker driven by a political or moral cause, not money
💡
Threat actors can also work in organised groups that sell their tools or skills to other criminals. This is sometimes called cyber crime as a service.

Outside and Inside Threats
External threats and insider threats, and why each one acts
Locked
🎯
Goal for this step

Define external and insider threats, and explain the motives behind each.

External threat

An external threat comes from outside the organisation. It is usually a criminal, a competitor, a nation, or a hacking group with no legitimate access.

Typical motives: money, stealing data, causing disruption, or spying. The Col-Tech attacker was an external threat actor.

Insider threat

An insider threat comes from inside the organisation: an employee, a contractor, or a former member of staff who already has access.

  • 1Malicious insider uses their access on purpose, often for revenge or money
  • 2Accidental insider makes a mistake, like clicking a phishing link or losing a device
  • 3Negligent insider ignores the rules, like reusing a weak password
⚠️
Insider threats are hard to spot because the person is meant to be there. That is why staff training and access control matter so much.

Key point: an external threat is outside the organisation; an insider threat is someone who already has access. Insiders can be malicious, accidental (a mistake) or negligent (breaking the rules).

Targeted or Untargeted?
Two very different styles of attack
Locked
🎯
Goal for this step

Explain what is meant by targeted and untargeted attacks.

Targeted attack

A targeted attack is aimed at one specific victim. The attacker does their homework first: they research the company, learn names, and write a message that fits. It takes more effort, but it is far more likely to work.

The Col-Tech attack was targeted. The attacker knew how the company operated and how to reach its staff.

Untargeted attack

An untargeted attack is sent out widely and hopes someone falls for it. The same fake email goes to thousands of people. It is cheap and random, like a spam text or a mass ransomware email.

🦊
Lumo says: as a rule, targeted attacks hit a chosen victim; untargeted attacks hit whoever clicks first. A targeted attack is usually more dangerous.
🎯 Targeted or not?
Tap the signs of a targeted attack.
📌
Key point: a targeted attack aims at one chosen victim and is researched first. An untargeted attack is sent out widely and simply waits for someone to click. Targeted attacks are usually more dangerous.

Where Are the Weak Spots?
Common vulnerabilities of people, businesses and nations
Locked
🎯
Goal for this step

List common cyber crime vulnerabilities of an individual, a business and a nation.

An individual

  • 1Weak or reused passwords, and no second step of sign-in
  • 2Oversharing personal details that help a scammer
  • 3Devices that have not been updated or backed up

A business

  • 1Unpatched software and no staff cyber training
  • 2Weak access control, so too many people can see too much data
  • 3No backups, and no plan for what to do if an attack happens

This also applies to charitable ventures and large multi-organisation services such as the NHS, where a breach can affect many organisations at once and even spread internationally.

A nation

  • 1Attacks on power, water, transport and banking systems
  • 2Spying on government and defence secrets
  • 3Attacks through shared supply chains and connected systems
💡
A vulnerability is a weak spot an attacker can exploit. A threat is what is trying to use it. A risk is how likely it is to hurt you.

📌
Key point: a vulnerability is a weak spot a threat can exploit. Individuals: weak passwords. Businesses: unpatched software and no training. Nations: poorly protected essential services. Shared services such as the NHS can spread the damage across many organisations.

Report It Straight Away
Why quick reporting limits the damage
Locked
🎯
Goal for this step

Explain why cyber security issues should be reported promptly.

If something looks wrong, the worst thing you can do is stay quiet. Reporting quickly is a skill, and it is one of the best defences a person or company has.

  • 1Contain the damage the sooner it is known, the sooner accounts can be locked and systems isolated
  • 2Protect other people a fast warning can stop the same attack hitting colleagues, friends or customers
  • 3Meet the law companies must report serious data breaches to the ICO within 72 hours
  • 4Keep the evidence early reports preserve clues that help find the attacker
  • 5Protect trust handling it well shows customers you take their data seriously
⚠️
Never try to fix a suspected breach alone and never hide it. Tell a teacher, an adult, or your IT team immediately.
I know a data breach should be reported to the ICO within 72 hours

Key point: report quickly to contain the damage, protect others, and meet the law. UK organisations must report serious data breaches to the ICO within 72 hours. Early reports preserve the evidence too.

Who Is Who in Cyber Security
Key organisations and the jobs that keep us safe
Locked
🎯
Goal for this step

Describe the role of key cyber security organisations and identify the general job functions of cyber security professionals.

Key organisations

  • 1NCSC the National Cyber Security Centre gives advice to UK organisations and helps during major incidents
  • 2ICO the Information Commissioner's Office enforces data protection law and can fine organisations that mishandle data
  • 3Action Fraud the UK national reporting centre for fraud and cyber crime
  • 4International bodies such as INTERPOL and ENISA help countries work together on cyber crime

General job functions

  • 1Monitoring watching networks for suspicious activity, often in a Security Operations Centre
  • 2Testing ethical pen testers deliberately attack a system to find weak spots first
  • 3Responding incident responders handle an attack while it is happening
  • 4Investigating digital forensics analysts examine devices and logs after an attack
  • 5Leading a Chief Information Security Officer sets the security strategy

Key point: remember the three organisations. NCSC gives advice and helps in major incidents. ICO enforces data protection law and can fine organisations. Action Fraud is the national reporting centre for fraud and cyber crime.

Hacking and Hat Colours
What hacking is, and the three hacker hats
Locked
🎯
Goal for this step

Define the term hacking, and explain the difference between white-hat, black-hat and grey-hat hackers.

Hacking means gaining unauthorised access to a computer system or network. Although people often picture a criminal, hacking is also done legally by security experts, who are paid to find problems before criminals do.

  • 1White-hat an ethical hacker with permission. They test systems and report the flaws so they can be fixed
  • 2Black-hat a criminal hacker. They break in without permission for money or harm
  • 3Grey-hat they break in without permission, but usually with no bad intent, and may report the problem afterwards
🦊
Lumo says: the colour is about permission and intent, not skill. A white-hat and a black-hat can have exactly the same skills, but only one has permission.
✨
Fun fact: many large companies run bug bounty programmes, where ethical hackers are paid a reward for finding a flaw responsibly so it can be fixed before criminals find it.
📌
Key point: hacking is gaining unauthorised access to a system. The colour is about permission and intent, not skill. White-hat has permission, black-hat is criminal, grey-hat breaks in without permission but without harmful intent.
🧠
